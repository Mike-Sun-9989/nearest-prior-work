# Borrowed Standing: How Corporate Investors Shape the Status of the Startups They Fund

Abstract. Startups lack track records, so outside audiences read their quality from the investors that back them. We argue that corporate venture capital (CVC) investors transfer standing to startups differently from independent venture capital firms. Because a corporate parent brings industry knowledge and complementary assets, its endorsement signals technological promise but also raises concerns about appropriation. Using data on 4,812 first-round investments in U.S. software and medical device startups between 2003 and 2018, we find that CVC backing increases the likelihood of a later prestigious independent investor joining the syndicate, but this benefit shrinks when the corporate parent competes in the startup's product market. The findings extend status theory by showing that endorsements carry the motives of the endorser, and they inform entrepreneurs choosing between corporate and independent money.

# Introduction

Young firms face a basic problem of evaluation. Customers, employees and later investors cannot observe a startup's capabilities directly, so they rely on observable affiliations. A large literature on status and endorsement shows that ties to prominent partners raise the perceived quality of an unproven organization and improve its access to resources. Most of this work treats the endorser as a neutral certifier whose only relevant attribute is its own standing.

Corporate venture capital complicates that picture. Established firms now take part in a large share of early-stage financing rounds, and they invest for strategic as well as financial reasons. A corporate investor can open distribution channels, share technical expertise and lend the credibility of a well-known brand. Yet it may also learn from the startup, steer its technology toward the parent's needs, or acquire it on favorable terms. Audiences who watch the startup therefore have reason to ask not only who invested, but why.

We develop the argument that the status a startup borrows from an investor is filtered through audiences' inferences about the investor's motives. We test it by studying whether corporate backing in the first round helps a startup attract a prestigious independent venture capital firm in later rounds. Our study contributes to research on interorganizational endorsements, to the literature on corporate venture capital, and to work on how entrepreneurs manage the resource providers they bring on board.

# Endorsements, Motives, and the Transfer of Standing

Status is a socially constructed ordering in which actors defer to others they regard as higher in quality. Because status is visible while quality is not, affiliation with a high-status actor works as a signal. When a prominent investor funds a startup, observers infer that the investor screened the venture carefully and found it promising, and they update their own assessment.

The signaling account assumes that observers read endorsements as sincere statements about quality. That assumption holds best when the endorser's payoff depends only on the startup's success. Independent venture capital firms come close to this ideal, since their returns come from exits. Corporate investors do not. Their payoff also includes strategic benefits that can be captured even when the startup itself does poorly, such as a window on new technology or a preferred path to acquisition.

We therefore expect that corporate backing transmits two messages at once. The first is a quality message. A corporate parent with deep knowledge of an industry is well placed to judge a technology, so its investment should raise observers' estimate of the startup's promise. The second is a motive message. Observers may suspect that the investment serves the parent's strategy more than the startup's growth, and that the parent may later restrict the startup's options.

Hypothesis 1. First-round backing by a corporate venture capital investor increases the likelihood that a prestigious independent venture capital firm joins the startup in a later round.

The weight of the motive message should depend on how much the parent stands to gain at the startup's expense. When the parent competes in the startup's own product market, the risk of appropriation is highest and the parent's interest in limiting the startup is clearest. Prestigious independent investors, who care about their own reputations and exit options, should then discount the endorsement.

Hypothesis 2. The positive effect of corporate venture capital backing on later prestigious investor entry is weaker when the corporate parent competes in the startup's product market.

# Data and Methods

We assembled data on first financing rounds of U.S. startups in software and medical devices from 2003 to 2018, drawing on a commercial venture database and hand-collected information on corporate parents. The sample includes 4,812 startups whose first institutional round we observe and whose subsequent financing history we follow for at least five years.

The dependent variable equals one when an independent venture capital firm ranked in the top decile of a network-based status measure joins the startup in any round after the first. We compute investor status with eigenvector centrality in the five-year co-investment network preceding each round. CVC backing is an indicator for at least one corporate investor in the first round. Product-market competition equals one when the parent reports revenue in the startup's primary four-digit industry.

We estimate Cox proportional hazard models of the time to prestigious investor entry, with controls for first-round size, founder experience, startup age, region, and industry-year fixed effects. Because corporate investors may select better startups, we also use an instrumental variable approach that exploits shifts in corporate venture budgets after parent-firm earnings surprises, and we match CVC-backed startups to independent-backed startups on pre-investment patents and founder backgrounds.

# Results

CVC-backed startups were 31 percent more likely to attract a prestigious independent investor within five years than comparable startups without corporate backing, supporting Hypothesis 1. The effect remains after matching and in the instrumental variable models. Consistent with Hypothesis 2, the effect fell by more than half when the corporate parent competed in the startup's product market, and it was not statistically different from zero for startups in the parent's core four-digit industry.

# Discussion

Endorsements are not only signals of quality. They are also statements by actors with interests of their own, and audiences read them that way. By showing that the standing a startup borrows from a corporate investor depends on the investor's competitive position, we connect status theory with research on the strategic motives behind corporate venture capital. For entrepreneurs, the results suggest that corporate money from a direct competitor may cost more in later fundraising than it brings in credibility.
